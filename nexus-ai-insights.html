At a glance
The product

ThreatModeler NEXUS lists every threat and security requirement for an app — often hundreds of them. Making sense of all that is a big part of a security architect's day.

The problem

To know how safe an app was and what to fix first, you had to scroll those long lists and piece the picture together yourself. It was slow, and easy to miss what mattered most. No competitor had anything like a shortcut.

Discovery

A problem the product team and I found from two directions.

This started as my own initiative — and the product team had independently flagged the same gap. I pulled in the people who'd have to build and stand behind it: TPMs, customer success, and engineering for feasibility. A few things were clear.

Finding 01

Users drown in the lists. Hundreds of threats and requirements, no fast way to see the overall posture — so the big picture got lost in the rows.

Finding 02

The real question is "what do I fix first?" Not "show me every threat." People wanted risk and urgency, not another exhaustive table.

Finding 03

The insight is the pattern, not the item. The value isn't a single threat — it's how they cluster, like external-to-internal pivoting toward the highest-consequence data.

Finding 04

A genuine differentiator. Competitors had no equivalent — this was something only we could put in front of customers.

The shift

From reading every list — to a posture read for you.

The model already knows all its threats and requirements. So instead of making people work the answer out in their heads, I let the tool do it: Insights reads everything and gives a short, plain summary of where the app stands and what to fix first. The full tables are still there when you need them — Insights is just the quick answer on top.

The model already holds the answer. Insights just reads it back to you — and says what to fix first.

01 · Deep dive — the decisions

Minimal on purpose.

The hard part wasn't generating a summary — it was deciding how little to show. The whole point is relief from density, so every decision pushed toward less.

A short read, not another dashboard

The obvious version is a wall of charts and counts. That just moves the overwhelm somewhere new. I chose a few sentences of plain language over a dense dashboard — the posture as a paragraph you can read, not a panel you have to parse.

Trade-off  A short summary hides detail by design. I kept the full tables one glance away, so depth is always there when it's needed.

End on the action, not the analysis

A posture that only describes leaves the user where they started. Insights closes on a single prioritised action — "start here" — so the read produces a decision, not just awareness.

Accessibility as a feature, not a checkbox

Audio translation and a loudspeaker mode let the posture be heard, not just read — useful for accessibility and for reviewing hands-free. Designing it in from the start, rather than retrofitting, made it part of what the feature is.

Keep the read live

A posture is only useful if it's current. Insights re-reads the model whenever the diagram changes, and can be refreshed on demand — so what you see always matches what you've built.

How I worked

Initiated it. Pressure-tested it. Shipped it.

A self-started idea only ships if the people who build and sell it believe in it — so I brought them in early, and took it to customers before calling it done.

  • Started it myself — and confirmed the product team had independently flagged the same gap, so it had a real mandate, not just enthusiasm.
  • Brought in the org — TPMs, customer success, and engineering for technical feasibility, plus developers for a smooth handover through the design system.
  • Explored 10+ directions before committing to the minimal summary.
  • Validated with customers — took it back to the same stakeholders, the product team, and our top three customers for usability and impressions, then implemented from what came back.

What shipped

A self-initiated AI panel that reads a threat model and returns a plain-language security posture with one prioritised action — live-refreshing, hearable, and something customers hadn't seen elsewhere.

What I'd measure next

Whether the posture changes what people do next — do they act on the recommended priority, and does time-to-first-remediation drop? Those are the signals I'd instrument to prove impact, not just adoption.
